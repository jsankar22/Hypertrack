# Nutrition Fundamentals for Training

## Energy balance
Body weight changes follow energy balance. Eating above maintenance calories produces weight gain, eating below produces weight loss, and eating at maintenance keeps weight stable. Maintenance is estimated from resting energy expenditure multiplied by an activity factor, then refined by tracking body weight over 2-3 weeks.

## Fat loss (cutting)
A moderate deficit of about 10-25% below maintenance is sustainable for most people. A useful target rate is 0.5-1% of body weight lost per week. Faster losses increase the risk of muscle loss, fatigue and poor training quality. Keep resistance training in place and keep protein high to preserve lean mass. Do not go below about 1200 kcal (women) or 1500 kcal (men) per day without medical supervision.

## Muscle gain (bulking)
A small surplus of about 5-15% above maintenance (roughly 250-500 kcal per day) supports muscle gain with limited fat gain. Aim for about 0.25-0.5% of body weight gained per week; beginners can gain a little faster, advanced lifters slower. Larger surpluses mostly add fat.

## Protein
For people who resistance train, about 1.6-2.2 g of protein per kg of body weight per day supports muscle growth and maintenance. During a calorie deficit, the higher end (2.0-2.4 g/kg) helps preserve muscle. Spreading protein over 3-5 meals, roughly 0.3-0.4 g/kg per meal (about 20-40 g for most people), is a practical approach. Good sources are meat, fish, eggs, dairy, tofu, tempeh, legumes and protein powder.

## Fat
Dietary fat should be at least about 0.6-0.8 g per kg of body weight, and typically makes up 20-35% of calories. Very low fat intakes can affect hormonal health and the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.

## Carbohydrate
Carbohydrate fuels high-intensity training and is the flexible part of the diet: it fills the calories left after protein and fat are set. Typical intakes are about 3-5 g/kg per day for moderate training and 5-7 g/kg or more for high training volume. Choose mostly whole-food sources (oats, rice, potatoes, fruit, bread, pasta, legumes) and time some around training if performance matters.

## Fibre, micronutrients and hydration
Aim for about 25-35 g of fibre per day, and 5 servings of fruit and vegetables. Drink to thirst and keep urine pale yellow; about 30-40 ml per kg of body weight per day is a common starting point, more in hot weather or heavy sweating.

## Practical habits
Plan protein at every meal, keep easy high-protein snacks available, weigh yourself several mornings per week and use the weekly average, and change calories in steps of 100-200 kcal when progress stalls for 2-3 weeks. Consistency over most days matters more than perfection on any single day.
